The Great Glitter Quiz-Off

A sparkling glossary game show, where the questions get sillier and the shimmer gets serious.

01. Glitter

Host:
Contestants, what is glitter: tiny mirrors, tiny rainbows, or tiny bits of party magic?
Answer:
Glitter is a collection of small, reflective particles. Their flat faces catch and bounce light in different directions, creating the familiar sparkle. “Party magic” is not a scientific definition, but it is an understandable one.

Contestant guess: “A miniature disco that escaped from a craft drawer.” The judges will allow the spirit of the answer, if not the science.

02. Facet

Question:
What makes one tiny speck flash brightly while the speck beside it looks almost dull?
Definition:
A facet is a flat surface on a glitter particle. As the particle tilts, its facets reflect light toward or away from your eyes. A moving crowd of particles therefore seems to twinkle, even though each piece is simply reflecting light.

Ridiculous guess: “They take turns pressing the sparkle button.” Not quite—but changing angles do create that on-and-off effect.

03. Hexagon

Question:
Which shape might make a glitter particle look like it was cut by a very tidy bee?
Definition:
A hexagon has six sides, and hexagons are common glitter shapes. Glitter can also be cut into squares, circles, stars, and other shapes. Shape influences how particles pack together and how their edges catch the light.

Audience shout: “A hexagon is a tiny stop sign for dust!” Creative, but the traffic department has not approved it.

04. Polyester Glitter

Question:
For the first increasingly absurd wager: what material is often used to make conventional craft glitter?
Definition:
Much conventional glitter is made from thin plastic film, often polyester, with reflective or colored coatings. The film is cut into small particles. Its lightness helps it cling to surfaces—and also helps loose glitter travel far beyond the table where it began.

Contestant guess: “It is shaved off a disco ball.” A spectacular image, but ordinary glitter is manufactured from film rather than harvested from dance floors.

05. Mica

Question:
Can a mineral make something shimmer without being the same thing as plastic glitter?
Definition:
Yes. Mica is a naturally occurring mineral that can be processed into thin, reflective particles. It is used in some cosmetics and decorative products. Mica-based shimmer and plastic glitter may look similar at a glance, but they are different materials with different properties.

Host’s note: “Sparkly” describes an appearance, not a recipe. Check product labels rather than assuming every shimmer is made the same way.

06. Cosmetic-Grade Glitter

Question:
Here comes a beauty-pageant brainteaser: is every glitter safe to put near your eyes?
Definition:
No. Products intended for cosmetic use are made and labeled for particular uses; craft glitter is not automatically suitable for skin, lips, or the eye area. Follow the product directions, keep particles out of the eyes, and use only products specifically intended for the area in question.
  • Check that the product is labeled for its intended cosmetic use.
  • Do not use craft glitter as makeup.
  • If a particle gets into an eye, avoid rubbing and follow appropriate medical guidance if irritation continues.

07. Biodegradable Glitter

Question:
Can glitter be designed to break down more readily than conventional plastic glitter?
Definition:
Some products marketed as biodegradable glitter use plant-derived materials, such as cellulose, as a base. “Biodegradable” does not mean “vanishes instantly anywhere,” though. Breakdown depends on the product and the conditions around it, so check the maker’s information and dispose of it responsibly.

Ridiculous guess: “It disappears at midnight, like a pumpkin.” Sadly, there is no confirmed evidence of enchanted deadline glitter.

08. Microplastic

Question:
Suppose a tiny plastic particle escapes a craft project. Why might its small size matter?
Definition:
Microplastics are very small plastic particles. Plastic glitter can contribute to plastic pollution when it escapes into the environment, where small particles can be difficult to collect. Using only what a project needs, catching scraps, and choosing suitable alternatives can help reduce avoidable release.

Contestant guess: “A microplastic is a plastic so small it needs a micro-sized passport.” The passport is fictional; the concern about tiny plastic particles is not.

09. Glitter Containment

Question:
For the grand practical round, how does a careful contestant keep a glitter project from becoming a glitter migration event?
Definition:
Containment means keeping loose particles in the work area and collecting leftovers instead of washing them down a drain. A tray, a damp cloth for cleanup, and a closed container can all help. Pouring with care is surprisingly useful, despite having no dramatic game-show soundtrack.

Three-point strategy:

  • Work over a tray or washable surface.
  • Return unused glitter to a sealed container.
  • Wipe up spills and put the collected material in the trash rather than rinsing it away.

10. The Glitter Afterlife

Final question:
What is the most ridiculous place a lost glitter speck might turn up?
Official answer:
There is no official record of glitter reaching the moon, infiltrating a submarine, or winning custody of a craft drawer. But loose particles can cling to clothing and tools and travel from place to place, which is why one project can seem to leave sparkles behind long after cleanup.

Lightning-round verdict: Glitter is a reflective material, a design choice, and—when scattered carelessly—a cleanup challenge. The champion’s move is to enjoy the sparkle while keeping it where it belongs.
