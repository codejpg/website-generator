Ananas: The Pineapple in the Spotlight

Two very different ways to meet the fruit with the crown.

Grandma’s Kitchen: A Fruit with Impossibly Good Manners

Now, gather round, dears, because Grandma has brought home an ananas. That’s another name for a pineapple, though I once thought it was the name of the polite little hat it wears. It does look dressed for a ceremony, with its spiky green crown perched on top. Don’t bow to it, though. I tried that once and nearly knocked over the fruit bowl.

The pineapple comes from a plant called Ananas comosus, and here is the surprise: it doesn’t grow on a tree. It grows close to the ground, among long, stiff leaves. I used to picture tiny pineapples dangling from branches like golden ornaments, but nature has apparently chosen a less convenient arrangement. Each plant usually makes one pineapple at the center, after flowering. The fruit forms from many small fruits joined together, which is why its skin looks like a collection of little tiles.

And that crown? It isn’t a leafy lid somebody forgot to remove. It can be planted to grow another pineapple plant, although patience is required. You may wait a good while for fruit, so don’t promise the grandchildren a pineapple harvest by next Tuesday. A plant can also be started from a side shoot, and commercial growers often use such shoots to raise new plants.

When choosing one at the market, I give it a sniff near the base. A sweet, fresh smell is a happy sign. I used to tug a leaf and declare that if it came out easily, the pineapple was ripe—like testing whether a hat fits. But that trick isn’t a dependable test. Better to look for a fragrant fruit that feels heavy for its size and doesn’t smell sour or fermented. Once cut, keep it covered in the refrigerator, and use a clean knife; the crown is for planting or compost, not for stirring the pudding.

Grandma’s small kitchen discoveries

  • Fresh pineapple is delicious in fruit salad, salsa, or alongside roasted vegetables.
  • Its enzyme, bromelain, can soften proteins, so fresh pineapple may make gelatin desserts refuse to set. Canned pineapple is heated during processing, which inactivates much of that enzyme.
  • The core is firmer than the juicy outer flesh, but it can be sliced thinly, blended, or cooked instead of tossed away.

So there you are: ananas is not a pinecone, not a tree ornament, and not a hat, although I reserve the right to call it the best-dressed fruit in the pantry.

Behind Door Number Three: The Tropical Plant Nobody Expected

Ladies and gentlemen, keep your hands poised above those buzzers! Behind door number three is not a miniature palm tree, not a citrus cousin, and certainly not a pineapple-shaped treasure chest. It is the ananas: a tropical plant in the bromeliad family, unveiling its grand prize right down near the ground!

That’s right, contestants: the familiar pineapple is the result of a flower cluster developing into a single joined fruit. Each of those patterned “eyes” on the outside marks one of the small fruits, fused together around the central core. What looks like one perfectly orchestrated prize is really a crowd of individual parts taking the stage as a team. Give that arrangement a standing ovation—or at least a careful rinse.

The plant’s story begins in South America, where pineapples were cultivated long before they became a dramatic centerpiece on dining tables around the world. European voyagers carried them across oceans, and growing the fruit in cooler places became a costly, elaborate undertaking. For a time, a pineapple could be displayed as a symbol of hospitality and extravagance. Imagine that: the guest of honor was also the dessert.

Today, pineapples grow in warm regions across the world, with major production in places including Costa Rica, the Philippines, and Brazil. They are often grown in fields in carefully managed rows. Their pointed leaves may look like a prickly little fortress, but the plant is not a tree and the fruit does not hang from a branch. The dramatic crown is a tuft of leaves, and under the right conditions it can begin the next act by growing into a new plant.

The host’s final reveal

What makes the ananas more than a pretty face? Its flavor balances sweetness with lively acidity, and the fruit supplies vitamin C and other nutrients. Its bromelain—a group of enzymes found especially in the stem and also in the fruit—breaks down proteins. That’s why fresh pineapple can tenderize meat, and why it can interfere with gelatin setting. In this round, science has beaten dessert to the buzzer.

Now for the suspenseful question: how can you tell whether a pineapple is ripe? Color alone does not provide a perfect answer, since varieties differ and a greenish fruit may still be ready to eat. Smell the base, feel for a little give without mushiness, and choose one that feels substantial. Once cut, its fragrant flesh can be eaten fresh, grilled, cooked into sauces, or blended into drinks. The plant may take months to produce its fruit, but the curtain rises the moment you slice it.

And that, dear contestants, is the grand reveal: the ananas is a ground-growing bromeliad, a many-part fruit, a tropical traveler, and a remarkably convincing argument for keeping a sharp knife handy. The audience goes wild—and Grandma asks whether the crown can be planted in her teacup.
